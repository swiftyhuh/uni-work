# Probability Theory

Many processes in nature, technology, economics, and everyday life involve uncertainty. We may know the set of all possible outcomes, but we cannot always determine in advance which outcome will actually occur.

_Probability in daily life_ ... _Will it rain tomorrow? According to the meteorological_ _forecast_ _, it will_ _probably_ _rain. Will I win my_ _bet_ _? I may have a good_ _chance_ _of winning, but there is also a_ _risk_ _of losing. Both_ _outcomes_ _are_ _possible_ _, and neither can be_ _predicted_ _with_ _certainty_ _._

Probability theory provides a mathematical framework for describing and analyzing random phenomena. Probability allows us to quantify uncertainty, assess risks, analyze data, make predictions.

- _Random_ means determined by chance: the particular outcome is uncertain and cannot be predicted with certainty in advance; it is unpredictable;

### Examples of applications in Computer Science

▶ Machine learning and artificial intelligence: Learning algorithms make predictions based on observed data. Since data are often incomplete, uncertain, or noisy, probabilistic models are used to quantify uncertainty and evaluate predictions.

Examples: spam filtering, image recognition, speech recognition, large language models.

▶ Randomized algorithms: Many efficient algorithms intentionally use randomness in order to improve their expected performance or simplify their design.

Examples: randomized Quicksort, randomized hashing, Monte Carlo algorithms.

▶ Computer networks: Randomness and uncertainty arise naturally in communication systems.

Examples: packet arrivals, network delays, transmission errors, network failures.

▶ Cybersecurity and cryptography: Modern cryptographic systems require high-quality random numbers. Examples: cryptographic key generation, initialization vectors, authentication protocols.

- Computer games: Randomness is used to make games less predictable and more varied.

Examples: random game events, procedural generation, randomized rewards, non-player character behavior.

### Random-number generation

Computers can generate sequences of _pseudo-random numbers_ using deterministic algorithms. Such sequences appear random, but they can be reproduced if the algorithm and its initial state are known. It is also possible to obtain _true random numbers_ from unpredictable physical phenomena, such as radioactive decay, atmospheric noise, or other sources of physical noise. For example, quantum theory allows us to describe the probability of radioactive decay, but not to predict the exact time at which a particular nucleus will decay.

### Geometric probability

The classical approach to probability is restricted to finite sample spaces. If the set of elementary outcomes is infinite, then there are events whose probabilities cannot be meaningfully defined by means of the classical definition. Geometric probability extends the classical notion of probability to situations in which the sample space contains infinitely many outcomes. In this setting, outcomes are represented by sets of points in a geometric region, and probabilities are determined by _geometric measure_ : _length_ in R, _area_ in R2 , and _volume_ in R3 . ## What is Bayes’ formula?

▶ Bayes formula, named after the British statistician, philosopher, and reverend Thomas Bayes (1702–1761), is used to compute conditional probabilities. In particular, it allows us to assess _how likely a possible cause is after observing its effects_ .

▶ It provides a systematic way to update an initial probability when new information or evidence becomes available.

▶ Typical applications include spam filtering, medical diagnosis, fault detection, pattern recognition, risk assessment, and probabilistic classification. For example, Bayes’ formula can be used to determine the probability that an email is spam given the words it contains, or the probability that a system has a particular fault given the observed error messages.

▶ Bayes’ formula is also a fundamental tool in machine learning, especially in Bayesian inference and probabilistic classification methods such as the naive Bayes classifier.
